basco._legacy.f2bptoolkit.covariance

Legacy compatibility reference. Prefer current BASCO workflows; copied implementation details are not new development targets.

Covariance analysis for the full two-body problem.

What this provides

The two matrices an orbit-determination problem is built from:

Phi(t) = d x(t) / d x(0)        state transition matrix, 30 x 30
Psi(t) = d x(t) / d p           parameter sensitivity,   30 x Np

where p is a chosen subset of the two bodies' mass parameters -- the inertia integrals T^lmn, including T^000 which is the mass. From those, the information matrix and its inverse follow directly.

The estimating of inertia integrals is what motivates all of this, and it is only possible because SimConfig accepts them directly. Perturbing T^200 alone corresponds to no ellipsoid, so there is no shape that could express the perturbed body and no way to compute the sensitivity by varying a shape.

Method, and why it is finite differences

GUBAS returns a trajectory and no derivatives of any kind. Analytic variational equations would need the second derivatives of the mutual potential, which do not exist anywhere in the C++ -- du_x, du_c and dT_dc are all first order. Building them means extending the Hou recursion by another differentiation order, which is a substantial piece of work in its own right.

Central differences need none of that and are available today. The cost is accuracy: roughly eps^(2/3) ~ 6e-6 relative in the best case, against machine precision for the analytic route. For covariance work that is usually irrelevant, because the model error and the measurement noise dominate it by orders of magnitude -- but it is a real limit and it is stated here rather than buried.

Fixed-step integrators only. This is not a style preference. An adaptive controller chooses its steps from the trajectory, so two neighbouring perturbed runs take different steps, and the difference of two differently-discretised trajectories is dominated by discretisation rather than by the perturbation. The result is not merely noisy, it is wrong, and it does not improve as the step size falls. variational_matrices rejects RK87 for this reason.

VariationalProblem.variational_matrices

variational_matrices(self, t_final: float, integrator=None, nOut: int=1, rel_step: float=1e-05)

Compute Phi and Psi by central differences.

Returns (times, Phi, Psi) with shapes (N,), (N, 30, 30) and (N, 30, Np).

rel_step is the fractional perturbation applied to each quantity. The default 1e-5 sits near the theoretical optimum for a central difference, where truncation error ~h^2 and rounding error ~eps/h cross at h ~ eps^(1/3) ~ 6e-6. Values far from it are worse in one direction or the other, which convergence_diagnostic will show.

_slot_scale(self, body, lmn)

Magnitude yardstick for perturbing an integral slot whose own nominal is zero.

The body's largest non-zero integral at the SAME total degree: siblings of one degree share units (kg m^l) and magnitude, so one of them is the right measure of how far to push a slot that is itself zero. Falls back to the largest anywhere in the cube when a whole degree is empty, which is the ellipsoid's situation at every odd degree.

VariationalProblem.convergence_diagnostic

convergence_diagnostic(self, t_final: float, integrator=None, steps=(0.001, 0.0001, 1e-05, 1e-06, 1e-07))

Sensitivity of Psi to the differencing step -- the honesty check.

A finite-difference derivative is only meaningful where it has a plateau: a range of step sizes over which the answer barely moves, bounded above by truncation error and below by rounding. Outside a plateau the number returned is an artefact of the step size, and no amount of downstream covariance algebra will reveal that.

Returns {rel_step: Psi_final}. Compare neighbouring entries; the flat region is the trustworthy one.

VariationalProblem.information_matrix

information_matrix(H_list: Sequence[np.ndarray], noise_cov: Sequence[np.ndarray], prior_information: Optional[np.ndarray]=None) -> np.ndarray

Accumulate Lambda = Lambda_0 + sum H^T R^-1 H.

H is the measurement partial with respect to the estimated vector, already including Phi and Psi -- typically (d y / d x) @ [Phi Psi].

R^-1 is applied by a Cholesky solve rather than by forming an explicit inverse: inv() on a poorly conditioned R squares its condition number for no reason, and R is usually small and well structured.

VariationalProblem.covariance

covariance(Lambda: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray

Invert the information matrix via Cholesky, with a real error on rank deficiency rather than a pseudo-inverse.

A pseudo-inverse here is actively dangerous: it silently projects out the unobservable directions and then reports a small variance along them, which reads as "this parameter is perfectly known" when the truth is "this parameter is not determined at all". If this raises, the system is telling you something real -- use :meth:observability to find out what.
